How-to guide

How to move a tiny home onto your land

Short answer

Get the approval first (council approval to live in it on wheels, or a building permit to put it on footings). Then check it is within the road limits of 2.5 m wide and 4.3 m high, walk the access route, prepare a level pad and have licensed trades connect power, water and sewer.

Last updated 30 October 2026

Step 1: Sort the approval before it arrives

Do this first, because it decides where the home can go and how long it can stay.

Your planApproval you need
Stay in it on wheels for a few nightsUp to 5 nights: the landowner's permission only
Live in it on wheels longer (for example while building a house)Council approval under the caravan rules, for up to 24 months at a time
Put it on footings as a homeA building permit before it is placed, and planning approval if it doesn't meet the standard rules

Without approval or an exemption, living in a caravan or tiny home on wheels outside a licensed park is an offence, on private or public land.

Step 2: Check it can legally travel

A tiny home on wheels is a trailer when it is on the road. Main Roads WA says a vehicle or combination over any of these limits needs a permit or order:

LimitWithout a permit
Width2.5 m
Height4.3 m
Length of a combination (tow vehicle plus tiny home)19 m

Most tiny homes are built to 2.5 m wide and under 4.3 m high so they can travel without a permit. Anything bigger is an oversize load, which needs a Main Roads permit and often escort vehicles. Western Power treats any load 4.3 m high or more as oversize, and it needs Western Power's authorisation too ($120 online in the Perth network) because of overhead power lines. The trailer generally needs to be licensed, and the tow vehicle must be rated for its weight. A specialist transport company will handle most of this.

Step 3: Check the site access

  • Width and turns. Walk the route from the street to the spot. Gates, side paths, trees and tight turns stop more deliveries than anything else.
  • Overhead. Look for power lines, eaves and branches along the route.
  • Ground. Soft sand or lawn may not hold a heavy trailer. Some deliveries need a crane instead, which needs a clear lift path and somewhere for the crane to stand.
  • The verge. Heavy vehicles can damage the kerb and footpath. Some councils require a verge permit or bond for building work.

Step 4: Prepare the pad and services

  • On wheels: a level, firm base, with the wheels kept on so the home stays movable. Council approval can require it to stay roadworthy and able to leave quickly, for example in a bushfire.
  • On footings: footings designed by an engineer, built under the building permit.
  • Services: power from the main switchboard by a licensed electrician, water by a licensed plumber, and sewer or an approved septic system. Council approval for a tiny home on wheels can require power, water, sewage and cooking facilities.

Step 5: After it lands

For a home on footings, the builder lodges the notice of completion (BA7) within 7 days of finishing. For a home on wheels, keep a copy of your council approval and diary the expiry date so you can re-apply in time.

Questions

Common questions

How wide can a tiny home be to tow without a permit in WA?

2.5 m wide and 4.3 m high. Main Roads WA treats anything over those limits as a restricted access vehicle that needs a permit.

Do I need council approval before delivering a tiny home?

If anyone will live in it for more than 5 nights, yes: council approval under the caravan rules for a home on wheels, or a building permit for a home on footings.

Can a crane lift a tiny home into my backyard?

Often, yes. You'll need a clear lift path, space for the crane to set up, and sometimes a council permit to use the street or verge.

Can I take the wheels off later?

Yes, but it then becomes a dwelling and needs a building permit, so it must meet the building code like any house or granny flat.
